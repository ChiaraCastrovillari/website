Selected Works In Progress

"What are the Labor Market Effects of U.S. State-Level R&D Tax Credits?"

Draft available upon request.

This paper exploits the staggered adoption of state-level R&D tax credits in the U.S. using a differences-in-differences design to estimate the effect of these incentives on the R&D labor market. The credits raise R&D employment by over 14% and increase the number of R&D establishments by about 4%, while having no effect on wages, with important implications for innovation and labor market policy.

"The Geography of Entrepreneurship: Evaluating the Role of Learning and Sorting across Locations," with Elio Nimier-David

Draft available upon request.

This paper examines how geography and career histories shape entrepreneurial entry and performance. Using French administrative data that link workers and entrepreneurs across employment and self-employment spells from 2006 to 2021, we reconstruct detailed career trajectories — including location, firm, industry, and wage histories — for a large sample of the French working population. We ask whether entrepreneurial individuals sort into cities, or whether living and working in larger locations induces entrepreneurship. We first document the timing of entrepreneurial entry around individual location changes using a movers design. We then estimate an extended Abowd-Kramarz-Margolis-style model that decomposes variation in entrepreneurial entry into individual ability, static location effects, and dynamic returns to accumulated city- and firm-size experience. Our goal is to disentangle sorting, city-specific and firm-specific experience that induces entrepreneurship. This speaks to the relative importance of learning versus sorting in explaining the concentration of entrepreneurship in large cities, with implications for place-based policy.

"Unpacking Agglomeration: Industrial Diversity and Knowledge Spillovers," with Eleonora Patacchini and Davide M. Coluccia

Draft available upon request.

This paper studies how exposure to industrial diversity in urban locations shapes the direction of innovation. Existing theories of agglomeration economies disagree on whether knowledge spillovers arise from specialization within an industry or from diversity across industries, but seldom address which specific technological relationships between inventors and their surrounding environment affect innovation. We develop new, technology-based measures of industrial relatedness by using large language models to extract the downstream technological systems and functional roles described in the text of 2.47 million U.S. patents issued between 1850 and 1950, classifying pairs of industries as technologically complementary or substitutable. We then exploit the staggered construction of bridges across the United States between 1920 and 1940 as a shock to the network of industries an inventor is exposed to, comparing inventors with different predetermined exposure to complementary versus substitutable industries around each bridge opening.
